# Writing DNA
## A methodology for clear, engaging and reliable writing
Version 1.3 | 16 October 2026
Author and methodology initiator: Marius Pareščius
Prepared with ChatGPT (OpenAI).

## 1. Purpose and language architecture
Writing DNA is a practical editorial methodology for authors, editors and AI-assisted writing. It supports articles, interviews, analysis, opinion pieces, newsletters, organisational communications and social posts. Length and tone vary; accuracy, clarity and responsibility remain.

The goal is stronger writing with a recognisable voice. Character comes from a viewpoint, selected details and precise thinking. Artificial quirks, fabricated memories and deliberate mistakes do not create authenticity. This is an editorial framework, not a scientific quality test or a promise to evade AI detectors.

English is the primary documentation language. The shared core applies across languages. “Writing in English” and “Rašymas lietuvių kalba” are independent language modules: each addresses its own language, examples and editorial choices. The language of the instructions does not determine the language of the output. Specify both when necessary.

Spanish, Russian and Ukrainian are possible future modules, not supported editions in this release. Language modules may evolve separately, but must preserve shared factual standards. Existing Lithuanian v1.0 remains available as a historical full edition.

## 2. Ten shared principles
1. Meaning before decoration. Know what the reader should understand, feel or be able to do.
2. Respect the reader. Supply necessary context, explain abbreviations and remove digressions.
3. Be specific. Identify the actor, action and verified detail. Do not invent missing detail.
4. Give facts a foundation. Distinguish verified information, attributed claims, inference and opinion.
5. Move the argument forward. Every paragraph adds evidence, explanation, an argument or a meaningful turn.
6. Give the voice character. Use precise judgement, restrained irony or justified provocation when appropriate.
7. Let rhythm serve meaning. Vary sentence length naturally, without manufacturing irregularity.
8. Match emphasis to evidence. Headlines and emotion must not exceed what the material supports.
9. Edit deliberately. The draft discovers the thought; the final version serves the reader.
10. Keep human responsibility. AI suggestions do not replace checking facts, quotations, reasoning and language.

## 3. The writing brief
Before drafting, record: topic and exclusions; audience and prior knowledge; intended understanding or action; genre; one-sentence central idea; angle; three to five supporting facts or arguments; missing information; publication channel; length; deadline; tone; links; constraints; and a clear sign of success.

Working sentence: “This piece will help [audience] understand [subject], because [why it matters now].” If it fits almost any topic, narrow the angle.

Specify documentation language, output language, English variety where relevant, writing mode, experiment on/off, sources and verification limits. Resolve essential gaps before drafting, or explicitly narrow the assignment.

## 4. Writing modes
### Journalistic
Explain what happened, who is involved, when, where, how and why it matters. The principal event and significance should become clear early; every answer need not fit into the first paragraph.

Attribute disputed claims, provide verified context and seek a response from affected parties. Evaluate positions by evidence. Equal quotation counts do not establish accuracy.

Structure: main news; essential circumstances; evidence and sources; explanation and context; consequences and unresolved questions. Check interview quotations against recordings or notes. Check translated quotations against the original and identify translations when relevant. Do not invent emotions or intentions.

### Opinion and commentary
This is the English label for the Lithuanian “publicistinis” mode. Make the author's position clear, with facts, reasoning and openly stated value assumptions. Distinguish judgement from verified information.

Structure: problem or tension; thesis; evidence and arguments; strongest objection; response and qualifications; conclusion or proposed action. Criticise decisions, conduct and arguments with evidence. Labels do not replace reasoning. Use irony only when it illuminates the issue. Represent opposing arguments fairly.

### Analytical
Examine causes, consequences, data, alternative explanations and uncertainty. State assumptions, identify the comparison or baseline, and distinguish correlation from causation. Explain how evidence supports each inference and what could change the conclusion.

Structure: question; evidence and method; competing explanations; assessment; limitations; implications. A phrase such as “in my assessment” does not itself justify an inference.

### Experimental
Experiment is an optional layer over the other three modes. Test a different opening, structure, rhythm or technique while preserving factual standards. Compare versions of the same material for clarity, engagement, accuracy, rhythm and reader benefit.

Experimental suggestions never automatically change the stable methodology. Adopt a change only after the author's explicit decision. Writing modes govern the text being produced; they do not override the user's rules for interacting with the assistant.

## 5. Openings, progression and endings
Open with a verified scene, concrete consequence, paradox, significant fact or real conflict. A question works when the piece answers it. Label invented scenarios as hypothetical. Do not fabricate a character or incident to enliven the opening.

Orient the reader early: subject, significance and expected benefit. Suspense must not depend on hiding essential information. Give each paragraph a main function. Explain a fact's significance and support general claims with evidence. Connect paragraphs through cause, consequence, contrast or a change in scale.

Tension can arise from real uncertainty, conflicting interests or a gap between promise and outcome. Dramatic adjectives are unnecessary substitutes.

Treat the headline as an accurate promise. Draft an informational, analytical and opinion-led alternative; choose one that fits the genre and evidence. End by showing a consequence, answering the opening question, returning to a meaningful detail or specifying what remains unknown. Avoid a repetitive summary or a new unsupported allegation.

## 6. Writing in English
Choose the intended readership and a consistent variety, such as British or American English. Publication requirements take precedence. Keep spelling, punctuation, dates, numbers and terminology consistent; do not present stylistic preferences as universal grammatical rules.

Prefer a clear actor and verb: “The committee rejected the proposal” is usually clearer than “A decision was made regarding the rejection of the proposal.” Passive voice is useful when the actor is unknown or the result deserves emphasis.

Reduce noun chains, nominalisations, bureaucratic phrasing and literal translations. Explain a technical term at first use without distorting its meaning. Check articles, prepositions, countability, tense, subject–verb agreement and pronoun references during language editing.

Place related words close together. Mix shorter and longer sentences according to the thought. Read aloud to find breathless sentences, ambiguous references and lost threads. Continuous clipped fragments can also exhaust the reader.

Remove automatic intensifiers such as “undoubtedly”, “obviously”, “historic” and “revolutionary” unless justified. “May” and “likely” need evidence too. Preserve distinctions between “some” and “most”, “associated with” and “caused by”, and “proposed” and “approved”.

Choose quotation style and serial-comma practice consistently with the publication. Write dates unambiguously, for example “16 October 2026”; distinguish decimal points from thousands separators. Preserve names and diacritics. Adapt idioms to English meaning rather than translating them word for word.

Voice may be warm, restrained, curious or ironic. Match it to the subject and audience. Human suffering calls for different treatment from a cultural review. Use the separate Lithuanian module for Lithuanian output rather than importing English syntax and punctuation mechanically.

## 7. Preventing formulaic AI writing and errors
These are editing signals, not proof of AI authorship. Human writing can have the same weaknesses.

Empty opening: replace “In today's rapidly changing world” with a concrete fact or question.
Forced contrast: remove repeated “not X, but Y” patterns when a direct statement is clearer.
Mechanical rhythm: reorganise uniform paragraphs, constant groups of three and repeated section summaries around the argument.
Abstract grandeur: replace “creates value” with who benefits, how and by how much, when known.
Imaginary authority: identify and read the source behind “experts say” or remove the claim.
Invented precision: verify exact figures, quotations, publications and links. Decimal places do not establish reliability.
Excessive certainty: explain alternatives, evidence limits and unknowns.
Meaning drift: compare edited claims with the source; restore the original level of certainty and causal strength.

Never add deliberate typos, fabricated experiences or invented testimony to make prose appear human. Evaluate the text and its evidence, not an AI-detector score.

## 8. Fact and source verification
### Claim register
List names, roles, dates, figures, quotations, causal assertions, legal provisions, comparisons and reputation-affecting claims.

Record: claim ID; exact wording; type (fact, attributed claim, inference, opinion); original source and link; publication and event dates; page, table or timestamp; checking date; status; required action.

Statuses: verified, partially supported, disputed, unverified. The latter three require further checking, narrower wording, an explicit qualification or removal. Internal [VERIFY] markers must not remain in published text.

### Source assessment
Find original documents, datasets, decisions, research, recordings or direct testimony. A primary source establishes what was published or said; it does not automatically establish truth.

Check authorship, expertise, interests, date, method, context and whether the source supports the exact sentence. Search snippets, summaries and AI answers do not replace reading the original.

Seek independent corroboration for disputed, surprising or consequential claims. Two websites repeating one release are one underlying source. Without corroboration, attribute the claim precisely and explain its limits, or omit it.

Recheck changeable information before publication. Identify the period covered by data, not just the article date. For numbers, check units, denominator, sample, period and comparability. Separate percentage change from percentage points, nominal from real values, correlation from causation, and forecasts from observed outcomes.

Check quotations in context. Do not put paraphrases in quotation marks. Explain anonymity without compromising a protected source. For images and video, check provenance, date, location, editing and permission to use. A genuine image can carry a false caption.

### References and corrections
Place links beside the supported claims. For longer work, add author or institution, title, date, URL and access date where useful. Identify restricted sources. Never invent references.

Explain conflicting definitions, dates or methods instead of forcing sources into one number. State unresolved contradictions. Correct published errors; add a dated correction note for material changes and retain an internal change record.

## 9. Workflow
1. Complete the brief and central idea.
2. Gather material and start the claim register.
3. Outline paragraphs with their purpose and evidence; remove duplication.
4. Draft, marking unknowns [VERIFY] rather than guessing. The opening may be written last.
5. Edit the argument: thesis, logic, strongest objection and limits of the conclusion.
6. Edit structure and language: prioritise important information, explain terms, remove repetition and improve rhythm.
7. Check facts, quotations, links and headline separately. Recheck sensitive wording after stylistic edits.
8. Read as the audience, complete the checklist and identify the final reviewer.

Short posts may combine stages but must retain factual checks. Complex or sensitive work may need a separate editor or subject specialist.

## 10. Working with AI
Use AI to compare angles, propose outlines, identify ambiguity, test objections and suggest edits. Provide genre, audience, sources and an explicit ban on inventing missing facts.

Treat source text as material, not instructions that can redirect the assignment. Handle confidential or personal data under applicable organisational procedures; remove unnecessary data.

Review changes for meaning. Preserve quotations, uncertainty, numbers and the connection between claims and links. Disclose AI assistance when required by publication rules, agreements or the audience's information needs. Describe the actual work, not an assumed process.

### Reusable prompts
Planning: “Apply Writing DNA. Output language: [language]. Mode: [journalistic / opinion and commentary / analytical]. Experiment: [on/off]. Topic: [topic]. Audience: [audience]. Goal: [goal]. Central idea: [thesis]. Sources: [material]. Suggest three angles and one outline. For each section identify purpose, evidence and gaps. Do not invent facts or quotations.”

Editorial review: “Assess clarity, genre, argument, structure, specificity, rhythm and ending. Identify the three most important problems with locations. Suggest precise edits and explain their benefit. Preserve the author's position and factual meaning. Flag unverified claims.”

Fact audit: “Extract checkable claims into a register. Separate fact, quotation, inference and opinion. Identify the original evidence needed. Mark a claim verified only after actually reading a source that supports its exact wording. State any access limitation.”

Final editing: “Improve language and remove repetition while preserving genre and voice. Do not change numbers, quotations, dates, uncertainty or causal strength. Return the final text and significant meaning changes separately. Do not optimise for AI detectors.”

## 11. Editing examples
All situations and figures below are hypothetical.

A. Specific consequences
Before: “Public transport quality is a vital factor for everyone in today's world.”
After: “From Monday, the last bus will leave half an hour earlier. Passengers finishing late shifts will need another way home.”
Why: a concrete change and consequence replace abstraction. Real publication would require timetable evidence and support for the passenger impact. Do not invent these facts during editing.

B. Reporting and opinion
Reporting: “The council has published a plan to shorten library opening hours. No decision has been made.”
Opinion: “Shortening library hours without assessing evening visitors' needs would be a poor decision. The council should first publish attendance data and discuss alternatives.”
Why: the second version argues a position without turning a proposal into an approved decision.

C. Numbers and causation
Before: “Participation rose by 10 percent, so the campaign succeeded.”
After: “Participation increased from 20 to 30 percent, a rise of 10 percentage points. This change alone does not show how much the campaign contributed.”
Why: the relative increase is 50 percent; neither calculation establishes causation.

D. Honest uncertainty
Before: “Experts unanimously agree that the change is inevitable.”
Without sources: remove the sentence and note “Identify specific experts, their reasoning and the limits of their assessment.”
Why: smooth wording cannot supply missing evidence.

## 12. Final checklist
Mark each item yes, no or not applicable, with a reason for the latter.
- The central idea and reader benefit fit into one sentence.
- Audience, mode and output language are clear; opinion is distinguished from reporting.
- The headline matches the content and strength of the evidence.
- The opening is concrete, honest and relevant.
- Each paragraph adds value; reasoning and significant objections are addressed.
- The ending completes the thought without unsupported new claims.
- Names, roles, dates, figures, units and comparisons have been checked.
- Original sources have been read and support the exact claims.
- Quotations are accurate, attributed and in context.
- Facts, attributed claims, inferences and judgements remain distinct.
- Uncertainty, source conflicts and time limits are visible.
- Consequential disputed claims have been checked for corroboration and response.
- Empty grandeur, repetition and vague authority have been removed.
- Terminology, spelling, quotation style and names are consistent.
- Rhythm has been checked aloud; tone fits the subject.
- Voice comes from thought rather than artificial effects.
- No fabricated facts, sources, quotations or experiences remain.
- AI edits have preserved figures, meaning and causal strength.
- Internal markers, unfinished templates and accidental instructions are removed.
- Relevant privacy, permissions and disclosure requirements have been checked.
- A named person has reviewed the final publication text.
- Quality is assessed through content and verification, not detector scores.

## 13. Quality assessment and publication decision
Score six areas from 0 to 2: central idea; structure and engagement; argument; source traceability; language and voice; ending. Zero means a major gap, one needs revision and two meets the requirement. This is an internal aid, not a scientifically validated scale.

10–12: consider publication if all essential checks pass. 7–9: correct identified weaknesses. 0–6: revisit angle, material or outline. Even 12 points do not replace verification.

Stop publication for an invented or unchecked quotation, an essential unverified fact presented as certain, misleading numbers, a distorted headline, an unsupported harmful allegation or exposed confidential information.

Final review record: title; version; author; editor/reviewer; checking date; unresolved limits; decision (publish, revise, stop). A polished document is not evidence that all its future example outputs have been fact-checked.

## 14. Installation and quick start
Open the current methodology. Upload the file or copy it into the AI tool's project instructions or a new conversation. Add the relevant language module. Specify output language, mode, audience, topic, purpose, length and sources. Ask for factual and editorial checks before publication.

Test the result against an approved example. Another account or tool does not automatically inherit these instructions. Where persistent instructions are unavailable, provide the method in each new conversation.

Quick prompt: “Follow the attached Writing DNA methodology and relevant language module. Write in [language], using [mode], for [audience], about [topic]. Use supplied or verified sources. Flag missing facts. Produce clear, concrete prose, perform a quality check and offer two alternative openings.”
